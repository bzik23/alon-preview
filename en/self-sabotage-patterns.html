Self-sabotage: why we stop ourselves just as things start to work out

Just as everything starts falling into place, something inside blows it up. Why the pattern keeps coming back and how to break free of it. Read more.

October 15, 2026 4-minute read By Alon Shemesh

There's one moment that comes up again and again for many people, even if they don't always notice it.

Something is finally starting to work out.

The diet is taking hold. The new relationship is getting serious. At work, people are starting to notice you. The plan that waited for years is finally getting off the ground.

And then, right there, something inside stops.

Suddenly you stop working out. You start an unnecessary fight. You put off the important meeting. You "forget" to send the email that could have changed everything.

From the outside it looks like a lack of motivation or bad luck.

From the inside it feels almost like an invisible hand pulling you back, just as you started moving forward.

That's called self-sabotage. And it has far less to do with laziness or weak character than it seems.

Self-sabotage isn't failure, it's a habit

It's easy to think of self-sabotage as a flaw.

"What's wrong with me that I'm ruining things for myself again?"

But in most cases it isn't a flaw, it's a pattern. And a pattern is something we once learned, usually to protect ourselves.

Try thinking about it this way: almost any behavior that repeats again and again, even when it hurts us, is doing something for us on the other side.

Avoiding progress spares us the fear of failure.

The fight at the wrong moment pushes away a closeness that makes us anxious.

Procrastination keeps us in the familiar place, even if it's a narrow one.

The subconscious isn't really trying to succeed or to fail. It's trying to stay with what it knows. And sometimes what it knows is exactly the place where we're stuck.

Why success is what sets off the alarm

It would make sense to expect that we'd sabotage ourselves when things are bad.

But very often the exact opposite happens. It's success, closeness and opportunity that trigger the inner brake.

Because real progress requires us to step out of the familiar zone.

Success also brings expectations. If I succeeded once, maybe people will expect me to keep going. Maybe I'll disappoint. Maybe they'll discover I didn't really get there fairly.

Closeness brings exposure. If I let someone get close, they can also hurt me or leave.

So sometimes it's easier to stop ourselves in advance. If I didn't really try, I didn't really fail. If I'm the one who ruined it, at least I'm in control.

Self-sabotage is the subconscious's crooked way of protecting us from the thing we want most, because it's also the thing that scares us most.

The sentences hiding underneath

Self-sabotage almost never arrives as a conscious decision.

It arrives through small sentences we tell ourselves, usually without noticing:

"It probably won't last anyway."

"People like me don't get there."

"If it's too good, something is bound to go wrong."

"Better not to get excited, so I won't be disappointed."

These sentences are limiting beliefs. They sound like facts about the world, but they're really old rules we learned at some point, and we keep running them on autopilot long after they've stopped serving us.

And here is the important point: the pattern doesn't define you. It's only a habit you once learned, and you can learn differently.

The work on changing patterns starts exactly there: recognizing the moment when the invisible hand pulls you back, understanding which fear it's trying to protect you from, and building a different response in place of the one that runs on its own.

Why "understanding it" isn't always enough

Many people who come to me already know their pattern.

They can say "I always do this." They've read, they've listened, maybe they even know where it came from.

And still, it comes back.

Because self-sabotage doesn't live only in the mind. It lives in the body, in emotion and in the automatic reaction that jumps in before thought even has a chance to step in.

That's why understanding alone usually isn't enough to let it go.

I'm Alon Shemesh, an emotional therapist and personal development coach, and I guide people who feel they keep stopping themselves right before the finish line. In one-on-one work I combine conversation with tools from NLP, guided imagery and inspiration cards. The goal isn't to "beat" the pattern by force, but to reach the place where it formed, calm the fear that keeps it going and make a different choice possible, even in moments where there used to be escape.

That's part of what I do in personal coaching: not supplying more motivation, but removing what's stopping you.

Questions I hear about self-sabotage

Is self-sabotage just a lack of motivation?

Usually not. People who sabotage themselves actually want very much to succeed, and that's exactly why it's so frustrating. The problem isn't how much you want it, but a fear or an old belief that triggers an automatic brake at the wrong moment. More motivation usually doesn't solve it, because it doesn't touch the root.

If I already recognize the pattern, why do I keep going back to it?

Because intellectual recognition is only part of the story. The pattern is also held on an emotional and physical level, and it kicks in faster than thought can stop it. Focused work helps change the reaction itself, not just your understanding of it.

Do you catch yourself stopping right before?

The first step isn't more willpower, but understanding exactly what triggers the brake.

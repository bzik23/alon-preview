Social rejection in adolescence: why is my child always the one reaching out to friends?

Is your child always the one reaching out to friends? Social problems in teens don't always look like being shunned. How to recognize social rejection and help. Read more.

November 1, 2026 6-minute read By Alon Shemesh

This article is written for women and men alike.

He sends a message in the friends' group chat and no one answers. He suggests getting together on the weekend, but everyone is busy. The next day he finds out that some of his friends did meet up, just without inviting him. And still, next time he'll once again be the first to call, send a message and try to organize a get-together.

For many parents, watching situations like this from the sidelines brings up pain and helplessness. On one hand, they want their child to find a place among friends. On the other, they worry that he's trying too hard to win attention and friendship.

Social rejection in adolescence doesn't always show up as open shunning or deliberate exclusion. Sometimes it happens quietly, through invitations that never come, messages left unanswered and an ongoing feeling that you have to work hard just to be part of the group.

Why is the need to belong so significant in adolescence in particular?

Adolescence is a time when the peer group takes on a central place in a teen's life. Alongside the desire for independence and some distance from parents, a significant need develops for recognition, acceptance and a sense of belonging.

Social and emotional changes in adolescence also affect relationships. Friendships that were stable for years may change, new groups form and teens begin to explore their place within their social environment.

When a teen feels he isn't a natural part of the group, he may interpret it as a sign that something is wrong with him. Thoughts like "Maybe I'm not interesting enough", "Why doesn't anyone call me?" or "What do I have to do so they'll want to be with me?" can affect his self-confidence and self-image.

It's important to remember that not every change in friendships signals rejection. Sometimes it's about shifting interests, friendships developing in different directions or a temporary period of drifting apart. The difficulty begins when the feeling of rejection becomes a recurring experience that affects the teen's behavior and how he feels.

When friendship becomes a chase: signs parents should recognize

One of the hallmarks of social problems in teens is the gap between the desire to belong and the effort it takes to maintain social connections.

Some teens keep initiating get-togethers even when their friends barely respond. Others agree to activities that don't suit them, give up what they want or avoid expressing a different opinion for fear of losing their place in the group.

A few behaviors are worth noticing:

  • One-sided initiative: the teen is almost always the one who calls, sends messages and suggests meeting.
  • Fear of being left out: he finds it hard to say no to friends, even when their requests make him uncomfortable.
  • Sensitivity to responses: an unanswered message or a get-together he wasn't invited to causes great disappointment.
  • Social people-pleasing: he changes his behavior or hides his opinion to win others' approval.
  • Damage to self-confidence: he starts to believe he isn't good enough or that no one really wants his company.

These behaviors don't necessarily point to a significant social problem. However, when they repeat over time and come with distress, it's worth finding out what the teen is going through.

Not all social rejection is shunning

When people talk about social problems in adolescent children, parents tend to think right away of shunning, humiliation or hurtful behavior from the peer group.

But there are also less visible situations. For example, a boy who is invited to get-togethers only when they're short a person, a girl who feels she's always the last choice, or a teen surrounded by friends at school who hardly ever sees them in the afternoon.

Social media also plays a part in this experience. Photos from get-togethers, shared videos and WhatsApp groups can expose the teen again and again to activities he wasn't invited to. At the same time, it's important to distinguish between deliberate rejection and friendships that aren't mutual, or social situations that can be interpreted in different ways. Not every get-together that happens without your child is an attempt to hurt him.

This distinction matters for parents too, because it allows them to respond appropriately and not amplify the feeling of rejection unnecessarily.

Why do teens keep chasing friends who don't reciprocate?

Sometimes parents think the solution is simple: if his friends don't treat him well, he should find other friends.

But for a teen who is afraid of being alone, the option of stepping away from the peer group can be scarier than continuing an unbalanced relationship.

There are several possible explanations for this. Some teens fear they won't manage to make new friends. Others believe that if they try hard enough, their friends will eventually accept them. Sometimes it's a people-pleasing pattern, in which the need for outside approval outweighs the ability to express wishes and set boundaries.

In situations like these, the difficulty isn't only about the friends' behavior but also about how the teen sees himself and his place in social relationships.

A healthy friendship doesn't require everyone to initiate equally often, but it does need to allow a sense of mutuality, respect and security.

How can you help a teen experiencing social rejection?

Parents' response can influence how the teen understands the experience and copes with it.

Listen without rushing to solve the problem

Sentences like "They're not worth it" or "Just find other friends" are said out of a wish to protect, but they don't always make room for the pain the teen is feeling.

Instead, you can ask: "How do you feel when you're always the one who reaches out?" or "Do you feel they want to get together with you too?" The goal is to help him express his feelings and understand what is really bothering him.

Strengthen their sense of self-worth

When a teen's sense of worth depends on friends' responses, every unanswered message can turn into a personal disappointment.

It's important to remind him that his worth isn't determined by the number of invitations he gets or how popular he is. You can encourage interests, activities and experiences that let him feel capable and valuable outside the peer group too.

Encourage additional social connections

Sometimes teens invest all their energy in a single group of friends, even when the relationships within it aren't fulfilling.

Clubs, sports, volunteering and other social settings can create opportunities to meet teens with shared interests, without pressure to form instant friendships.

Teach them to set boundaries in friendships too

The ability to say "no", express a different opinion or choose not to take part in a certain activity is an important part of developing healthy relationships.

A teen who understands he doesn't have to give up on himself in order to belong may feel more secure in his relationships.

When does social rejection call for emotional support?

Not every social difficulty requires professional intervention. Many teens experience changing friendships, disappointments and moments of not belonging as part of growing up. However, when social rejection comes with prolonged withdrawal, avoiding school, a significant drop in self-confidence or ongoing emotional distress, it's worth considering getting help.

Emotional therapy for teens can help adolescents identify thinking and behavior patterns that make relationships hard for them, strengthen their sense of self-worth and develop ways of coping with challenging social situations.

When it involves shunning, harassment or ongoing social harm, it's important to involve the school staff as well and act to stop the harm, rather than leaving the teen responsible for coping alone.

You don't have to chase friends to be worthy of friendship

One of the most important messages parents can pass on to their teens is that friendship isn't supposed to be an ongoing test, in which you have to prove again and again that you deserve to be part of the group.

Social rejection in adolescence can be a painful experience, but it doesn't define who the teen will become. Through listening, building self-confidence, developing social skills and the right support, you can help them build relationships in which they feel wanted, valued and more secure.

The goal isn't to make all the friends like the teen, but to help him feel more secure in himself, recognize the relationships that are good for him and discover that he doesn't have to give up who he is in order to belong.

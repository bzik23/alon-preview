Low self-confidence in adults: why it happens and how it can change

You want to share your opinion in a meeting, but you're afraid people will think you're wrong. You find it hard to say "no" even when something doesn't suit you, or you compare your achievements to other people's. Sometimes you know exactly what you want, but when the moment comes, self-doubt stops you.

How to recognize it Where it comes from How confidence is built How it works Frequently Asked Questions
Before we begin

You can't always see it from the outside

Low self-confidence in adults isn't necessarily something you can see from the outside. You can be successful, run a family, hold a senior position and look sure of yourself, and still feel that you're not good enough.

When these feelings affect your choices, your relationships and your everyday life, therapy for low self-confidence can help you identify the thought patterns that hold you back, strengthen your self-worth and develop new ways of handling situations that bring up doubt and fear.

Alon Shemesh's emotional support offers a personal, attentive space where you can understand what lies behind the lack of confidence and work gradually toward change.

Signs worth knowing

How do you recognize low self-confidence in adults?

Low self-confidence doesn't only show up as shyness. Sometimes it appears precisely in sociable, responsible, successful people who feel they have to prove themselves again and again. The signs can show up in different areas of life.

💼

At work and in your career

Difficulty voicing an opinion, asking for a raise, taking on responsibility or handling criticism.

💞

In relationships

A constant need for approval, fear of rejection and a tendency to give up your own needs to keep the relationship going.

👥

In social situations

Comparing yourself to others, feeling inferior or worrying about embarrassing yourself.

⚖️

In decision making

Long deliberations, fear of getting it wrong and difficulty trusting your own judgment.

🚧

In setting boundaries

Difficulty saying "no", people-pleasing and feeling guilty when you choose yourself.

🪞

In how you see yourself

Excessive self-criticism, a feeling that you're not good enough and difficulty acknowledging your successes.

When these patterns repeat over time, they can narrow your sense of freedom and lead you to pass up opportunities you would have liked to take.

Where it comes from

Low self-confidence in adults: where does it come from?

Many people believe self-confidence should grow naturally with age and experience. But sometimes it is precisely the experiences gathered over the years that keep shaping the way we see ourselves.

Childhood experiences and family environment

The way we were treated as children can affect how we value ourselves. Repeated criticism, very high expectations or a lack of positive encouragement can contribute to a feeling that you have to work hard to deserve appreciation.

Social rejection and experiences of failure

Being shunned, rejected, humiliated or experiencing significant failures can leave a mark well into adulthood. Sometimes a person keeps fearing criticism or rejection even when their current reality is different from what they went through in the past.

Constant comparison to others

In a world where we are exposed nonstop to other people's successes, it's easy to focus on what we lack. Repeated comparison can deepen a sense of inferiority and weaken self-esteem.

Limiting beliefs and negative thought patterns

Thoughts like "I'm not good enough", "everyone is more successful than me" or "if I make a mistake, they'll think I'm a failure" can, over time, become automatic thought patterns.

The encouraging news is that even patterns that have been with us for years are not necessarily fixed. You can learn to recognize them and practice other ways of thinking and responding.
An important distinction

What is the difference between low self-confidence and low self-esteem?

Although they are connected, they are not quite the same thing.

Self-confidence refers to our belief in our ability to handle tasks, decisions and different situations. A person can feel very confident professionally, yet feel insecure in a relationship or in social situations.

Self-image refers to the way we perceive and value ourselves. Low self-esteem in adults can show up as an ongoing sense of worthlessness, self-criticism and difficulty acknowledging strengths and successes.

So improving self-confidence isn't just about learning to look more confident on the outside. It's also important to address how a person talks to themselves, values their abilities and deals with mistakes.

How confidence is built

How is self-confidence built? Changing patterns and setting boundaries

Self-confidence isn't a trait you're either born with or without. It's shaped by experiences, beliefs and what you go through in life, so it can be developed in adulthood too.

One of the important stages in the process is changing patterns that reinforce the lack of confidence. For example, someone who regularly avoids voicing an opinion out of fear of criticism never gives themselves the chance to discover that they can handle disagreement too. By identifying the thoughts that trigger the avoidance and gradually practicing new responses, a sense of capability can develop.

Another significant element is setting boundaries. People with low self-confidence may find it hard to turn down requests, express their needs or stand their ground for fear of hurting others. Learning to set healthy boundaries lets a person recognize that their own wishes, feelings and needs have a place too. It doesn't require confrontation or distancing yourself from people, just clearer and more respectful communication.

Work on empowerment and personal growth usually goes hand in hand with building confidence: the more we reconnect with our own wishes and abilities, the easier it becomes to act from self-worth rather than from fear.

Practical steps

How do you raise your self-worth and improve your self-confidence?

Improving self-confidence is a gradual process, not a change that happens overnight. Sometimes it's small, consistent steps that allow you to build a more stable sense of confidence.

  1. Recognize the critical voice: notice the thoughts that come up before challenging situations. Do you assume in advance that you'll fail? Recognizing those thoughts is the first step in examining them.
  2. Acknowledge your strengths and successes: notice small achievements too, your personal abilities and times you handled a challenge well, not only what didn't work out.
  3. Set small, realistic goals: instead of expecting dramatic change, choose one action you'd like to take - voicing an opinion, asking for something that matters to you, or facing a situation you've been avoiding.
  4. Practice setting boundaries: start with simple situations where you can say what works for you and what doesn't. Every experience like this strengthens the feeling that you have the right to choose.
  5. Develop a respectful attitude toward yourself: self-confidence doesn't require perfection. Being able to accept mistakes and treat yourself with compassion is a significant part of building more stable self-worth.
How it works

Therapy for low self-confidence with NLP and guided imagery

Emotional support with Alon Shemesh combines personal conversation, listening and tools from the world of NLP, alongside guided imagery and work with limiting beliefs. At the start of the process, you identify the situations where the lack of confidence appears, the thoughts that come up and the behavior patterns that keep reinforcing it.

Later on, you can work on changing thought patterns, strengthening your personal resources and practicing new ways of responding to challenging situations. Guided imagery can help with practicing relaxation and preparing mentally for situations that cause worry, and NLP tools are woven into the work on your inner voice, your personal interpretations and identifying beliefs that limit your sense of capability.

The goal isn't to become someone who fears nothing, but to develop the ability to trust yourself more, handle criticism and act even when doubt is present.
When to reach out

When is it worth seeking emotional support to strengthen self-confidence?

We all have moments of insecurity, especially when facing new situations. But when the fear of criticism, the need for approval or the feeling that you're not good enough stays with you over time, it's worth considering getting support.

Support for building self-confidence may be a good fit when the difficulty makes you avoid opportunities, settle in relationships, struggle to set boundaries or live with a constant feeling that you have to prove yourself. Sometimes it appears together with difficulty making decisions, when self-doubt makes it hard to trust your own judgment.

There's no need to wait until low confidence affects every area of your life. Sometimes the very decision to look at the patterns holding you back is the beginning of change.

A closing thought

Improving self-confidence: the first step

Self-confidence doesn't mean being sure of every decision or succeeding at everything. It also shows up in the ability to recognize your worth, deal with mistakes and keep going without letting fear run your choices.

Emotional therapy for teens and adolescents: understanding the difficulty and finding a new way forward

Your son doesn't open up the way he used to? Your daughter shuts herself in her room, pulls away from friends or reacts with anger to every attempt to get close? It isn't always easy to know whether this is normal adolescent behavior or a sign of emotional distress. Emotional therapy for teens gives young people a safe space, someone who listens and real tools for coping with the challenges on their mind.

Introduction

Adolescence: a time of change, challenges and pressure

Adolescence is a time of rapid change, and today more than ever, teens have to deal with social expectations, constant comparison on social media, emotional overload and the wish to find their place in the world. They don't always know how to explain what they're going through - sometimes the distress shows up as withdrawal, angry outbursts, difficulty making connections or a significant change in behavior.

Emotional therapy for teens and adolescents can give them a safe space, someone who listens and tools for coping with the challenges on their mind. At Alon Shemesh's Nefeshlava clinic, the support is based on tools from NLP and personal coaching, tailored to the needs of each teen and with the parents involved in the process.

Challenges in adolescence

Adolescence today: why is it so hard to be a teenager?

Adolescence has always been a complicated time, but today's reality adds challenges that didn't exist with the same intensity in the past.

Alongside the physical and emotional changes, teens deal with social pressure, a constant need to belong, academic expectations, questions of personal identity and a desire for independence. Social media creates a world where comparison with others is almost nonstop: photos, comments, WhatsApp groups and follower counts can affect self-image and the sense of belonging.

Long hours in front of screens can also become a way to avoid dealing with difficulties, loneliness or unpleasant feelings, and can sometimes hurt sleep, friendships and daily routine. Through all of this, teens are trying to build an independent identity, yet they still need support, boundaries and a sense of security.

The hard part is that they don't always know how to ask for help, and sometimes, just when they need it most, they pull away from the people closest to them.

How to recognize it

Signs of distress in adolescence: when should you seek help?

Not every change in mood points to an emotional problem. Temporary withdrawal, a wish for privacy or arguments with parents can be a natural part of growing up. Still, when changes are significant, lasting or start to affect day-to-day functioning, it's important to pay attention.

🚪

Withdrawal and distancing

Spends more time alone, avoids family conversations or stops taking part in activities they used to enjoy.

🎢

Sharp mood swings

Unusual irritability, crying, anger, frustration or rapid shifts between emotions for no clear reason.

🤝

Social difficulties

Avoiding meeting friends, loneliness, trouble fitting into a group or fear of rejection.

💔

Lower self-confidence

Repeated statements like "I'm worthless", "Nobody likes me" or "I can't do anything right".

⚡

Changes in behavior

Outbursts, unusual defiance, avoiding tasks or patterns that weren't typical of the teen before.

😴

Changes in sleep and appetite

Trouble falling asleep, ongoing fatigue or a significant change in eating habits.

📱

Problematic screen use

Difficulty disconnecting from the phone or computer and drifting away from other activities, to the point of disrupting daily routine.

📉

Lower functioning

Missing school, a significant drop in motivation or giving up on interests.

The longer the signs last, the more they appear together or the more areas of life they affect, the more important it is to seek professional advice. Talk of not wanting to live, self-harm or risky behavior requires an urgent assessment by a licensed mental health professional, not emotional support alone.

Where it helps

How can emotional support help teens? Four main areas

Emotional support for teens doesn't focus only on the behavior you see from the outside, but also on the feelings, thoughts and needs that may lie behind it. At the Nefeshlava clinic, Alon Shemesh focuses on four main areas.

Social problems in adolescence: when a teen feels they don't belong

For teens, friends are a big part of their world, and a sense of social belonging affects self-confidence and mood. Social problems can show up as difficulty making friends, weak social skills, loneliness or fear of rejection, and sometimes as ostracism, humiliation or social exclusion that deeply hurt self-worth. The support focuses on building self-confidence, developing communication skills, setting boundaries and building a sense of capability in social situations. In cases of ongoing social harm, it's important to involve the parents and the school as well, rather than leaving the teen to cope alone.

Difficulty with emotional regulation: when feelings take over the reaction

Some teens react intensely to situations that seem fairly simple to their parents: a small remark leads to an angry outburst, an argument with a friend ends in tears or withdrawal, and a moment of frustration turns into a feeling that everything is falling apart. Poor emotional regulation can show up as emotional flooding, outbursts, impulsiveness or difficulty calming down after a stressful situation, and sometimes the teen feels guilt or frustration over their own reaction. In the process we work on identifying the feelings and the situations that trigger them, becoming aware of automatic reactions and practicing new ways to deal with anger, fear and frustration - not to prevent feelings, but to learn to express them without getting hurt and without hurting others.

Changing behavior patterns: stepping out of a cycle that keeps repeating

Sometimes parents notice their teen returning to the same behavior again and again, even when it hurts them: a boy who gives up in advance for fear of failing, a girl who pleases her friends so she won't be left alone, or a teen who reacts with anger every time they feel criticized. Patterns like these sometimes develop from past experiences, limiting beliefs or coping strategies that became habits. Using tools from NLP, we work on identifying the patterns, understanding the situations that set them off and practicing new responses - working on limiting beliefs, reframing, guided imagery and strengthening a sense of capability. The goal isn't to change the teen's personality, but to let them choose how to respond instead of acting on autopilot.

Emotional guidance and support for teens: a safe place to talk and grow stronger

Not every teen dealing with an emotional difficulty needs help because of a defined problem. Sometimes it's a period of insecurity, confusion, trouble adjusting, tension at home or a general feeling that something isn't working. In situations like these, emotional support for teens offers a space to talk freely, get to know their feelings and thoughts and develop coping tools. The support focuses on strengthening self-image, developing self-awareness, identifying strengths and building a sense of capability and independence, and it's tailored to the teen's age, personality and the goals they want to reach.

How it works

How does emotional support for teens work with NLP and personal coaching?

Teens don't always connect with a direct conversation about their feelings. Some find it hard to explain what they're going through, and others worry they'll be judged or that someone will try to change them.

That's why the foundation of the process is building a relationship based on trust, listening and respect for the teen's world. In his work, Alon Shemesh combines tools from NLP (Neuro-Linguistic Programming) and from personal coaching, which focus on the connections between language, thoughts, feelings and behavior. It's an experiential, emotional approach rather than one protocol for everyone, and it's especially suited to teens who find it hard, or simply don't want, to "talk about feelings". The process may include:

  1. Identifying the difficulty: understanding the situations that challenge the teen and how they experience them.
  2. Working on thinking patterns: identifying limiting beliefs and exploring other ways of thinking.
  3. Strengthening personal resources: identifying strengths, past successes and existing abilities.
  4. Practicing hands-on tools: guided imagery, reframing and other NLP tools, as needed.
  5. Setting personal goals: working on changes the teen wants to make in their everyday life.

Every teen comes with a different emotional world, so the process is tailored to their personal needs and level of cooperation. NLP and personal coaching can serve as tools for developing skills and self-awareness, but they are not a substitute for evidence-based mental health treatment when it is needed; the research evidence for the effectiveness of NLP in treating mental disorders is limited.

The parents' role

What is the parents' role in the emotional support process?

When a teen is struggling emotionally, parents often feel helpless too. They want to help, but don't always know whether to ask questions, give space, set boundaries or insist on talking. Parental involvement plays a central role in supporting teens, because home is one of the most significant environments in their lives.

In a tailored emotional support process, parents can be involved as needed, helped to understand the teen's difficulties and to develop ways of communicating and offering support at home. At the same time, it's important to protect the teen's privacy and sense of independence, so they feel safe to share and to be partners in the process. The goal isn't to put the responsibility on the parents or on the teen, but to create a supportive environment that makes coping easier.

When there's resistance

Your child doesn't want to come to emotional support: what can you do?

Many parents can see their teen needs help, but run into resistance: "I don't need therapy", "I don't have a problem" or "Just leave me alone". In cases like these, it's best not to present the support as a punishment or as an attempt to fix something wrong with your child. Instead, you can open with a calm conversation, share your concern without blaming and ask what might help them feel better.

It's important to give the teen room to express their opinion, explain the purpose of the process and let them take part in the decision. Sometimes an initial consultation between the parents and a professional can help them understand how best to approach the subject.

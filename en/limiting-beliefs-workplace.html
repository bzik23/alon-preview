The limiting beliefs that stop good employees from moving forward

The most talented person on the team didn't apply for the role, and it isn't skills they lack. What is really holding them back? Find out more

August 30, 2026 6-minute read By Alon Shemesh

A new role has opened up on the team, and you already know who could be right for it.

She's skilled, respected, knows the organization and has already proven she can take on responsibility. The internal posting closes, and her name isn't on the list of candidates.

At the end of a meeting, you ask her why she didn't apply.

"I'm not sure I'm there yet," she answers.

It's easy to hear modesty or professional judgment in that sentence. Sometimes that really is the case. But sometimes something else hides behind it: not an objective assessment of her chances, but a conclusion she has already reached about herself.

"I'm still not good enough."

"I'm not one of the people who lead."

"I don't want to push myself forward."

"I need to know everything before I can take on responsibility."

These are no longer just thoughts about the role that just opened up. They're inner rules that can shape how a person interprets opportunities, risks and decisions.

And when they come back again and again, an excellent employee can stay one step behind their true ability for years.

Sometimes what looks like being stuck in your career starts somewhere else entirely

I meet this point in my one-on-one work too.

People don't necessarily come to me because they're "not succeeding at work." Sometimes it's quite the opposite. They're good, responsible, skilled and valued employees.

But something isn't moving forward.

They stay for years in a role they've outgrown. They avoid asking for a raise. They don't apply for the role they want. They find it hard to speak up in meetings even though they have something to say. They keep putting off a career change they've been thinking about for a long time.

From the outside, it's easy to call this a lack of confidence.

But a lack of confidence is sometimes only the visible result.

Underneath it there may be deeper patterns and beliefs: fear of rejection, a need to be perfect before taking a step, difficulty taking up space, worry about disappointing others, fear of making mistakes, or a belief that our worth depends on succeeding at everything we do.

As an emotional therapist and personal development coach, I also work with people who feel this kind of stuck in their working lives. The goal isn't to tell them they "just need to believe in themselves," but to understand what really triggers the freeze and build a practical path that lets them start moving differently.

What is a limiting belief, and how does it show up at work?

A limiting belief is a perception a person holds about themselves, about others or about how the world works, one that narrows the options they allow themselves to consider.

The problem isn't the mere existence of a thought like "I'm not good at presentations." We all have assessments of our strengths and weaknesses.

The question is what happens after it.

If the thought leads to "I need to practice more before the next presentation," this is a difficulty you can work with.

But if it turns into "I'm just not a presentations person," a skill has become an identity.

That's a significant difference.

A skill can be learned. Experience can be gained. Behavior can be changed.

But when a person describes a limitation as part of who they are, they may stop checking whether it's still true.

What do limiting beliefs sound like at work?

Limiting beliefs almost never come with a sign that says "this is a limiting belief."

Sometimes they even sound very responsible.

"I'm not a presentations person"

A skill is described as a personality trait.

Instead of saying "I find it hard to present in front of people right now," the person tells themselves that it's simply not who they are.

And that sentence affects much more than the upcoming presentation. It can lead them to avoid roles that call for presence, presenting or leading.

"Once I finish this project, I'll think about a promotion"

Sometimes that's a perfectly reasonable decision.

But if every project is followed by another project, and another reason to wait, it's worth checking whether the workload is really what's stopping the step, or whether it lets us put off a decision that scares us.

"I feel awkward asking"

A small sentence that can cost a lot over an entire career.

Awkward to ask for a raise.

Awkward to ask for help.

Awkward to say the workload is too much.

Awkward to put yourself forward as a candidate.

Awkward to say "I want this role."

Behind all of these, a much broader belief may be hiding: that my needs are a burden on others, or that taking up space means taking something away from someone else.

"There's no point, they've already decided anyway"

Maybe they really have already decided.

But sometimes we'd rather know in advance that the answer will be "no" than risk hearing it.

If I didn't apply, I didn't fail.

If I didn't ask for a raise, no one turned me down.

If I didn't present the idea, no one rejected it.

Avoidance protects us in the short term, but it also keeps us exactly where we are.

When is it no longer just a professional consideration?

It's important not to turn every hesitation into a limiting belief.

Sometimes an employee really isn't ready for the role yet. Sometimes they simply don't want to manage. Sometimes changing jobs right now isn't right for them.

The interesting inquiry begins when the choice keeps repeating itself.

When the same person wants to advance, but doesn't apply.

Wants to leave, but every year finds a new reason to stay.

Wants to speak up more, but in every meeting waits for the right moment that never comes.

Knows they need to set a boundary, but says yes again and again.

In situations like these, there's a gap between what I really want and what I actually manage to do.

And that gap is often where the real work begins.

In therapy and personal coaching, we can go into the person's specific pattern: understand what stops them, what triggers them, where the pattern shows up in their life, and build practical steps that make movement possible.

Maybe you don't need more motivation

If you feel professionally stuck, it's easy to think something is missing.

More confidence.

More experience.

More motivation.

Another course.

Sometimes that really is what's missing.

But sometimes you already have far more ability than you allow yourself to use, and that's exactly the starting point of personal empowerment.

I'm Alon Shemesh, an emotional therapist and personal development coach. In my work, I also guide people who feel stuck in their work and careers: people who want to advance, make a change, set boundaries, take up more space, or simply feel they're no longer running their careers out of the same fears and patterns.

The work focuses on identifying what's holding back movement, understanding the patterns and beliefs beneath the surface, and translating that understanding into steps you can actually start taking.

If you feel there's a gap between where you are today and where you know you can get to, that's exactly the place to start.

Questions I hear about feeling stuck at work

How do I know whether I'm really not ready for a promotion, or just afraid?

You don't have to choose between the two right away. You can start with a concrete question: what exactly am I missing? If you can name a skill, experience or knowledge that's missing, you have something to work with.

Do you need to leave your job to get unstuck?

Not necessarily. Sometimes the workplace really doesn't allow for growth, and the right change is to move on. But if the same pattern repeats across several different jobs, changing companies alone won't necessarily change it. You can move to a new office and take exactly the same fear with you.

Do you recognize yourself in this gap?

The first step isn't more motivation, but understanding exactly what's holding you back.

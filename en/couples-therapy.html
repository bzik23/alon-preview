Couples therapy for partners - pause, understand and build a better relationship

Do you feel like you're talking, but not really managing to understand each other? Simple conversations turn into arguments, recurring issues stay unresolved, and sometimes it seems the person who was closest to you is drifting further away. Couples therapy lets you step out of the routine, look at what's happening inside the relationship, and explore how to change what no longer serves you.

Before we begin

A good relationship isn't one without disagreements

A good relationship isn't one without conflict. It's one where you can talk about the hard things too, listen, understand, and find a shared way to face the challenges.

Not every crisis starts with a big event. Sometimes it's small changes that build up over time: fewer conversations, more criticism, a feeling that you're not being heard, or the same arguments over and over. The more the pattern repeats, the harder it is to believe that one more conversation will lead anywhere different.

Couples therapy lets you stop for a moment, look at what's happening inside the relationship, and explore how to change communication and behavior patterns that no longer serve it. Alon Shemesh, a certified NLP coach from Rehovot, works with a personal, attentive and respectful approach that focuses on recognizing patterns, improving communication and developing practical tools for your relationship.

The goal isn't to decide who's right and who's wrong, but to help both of you better understand your own needs, feelings and reactions, and those of your partner.
When to reach out

When should you seek help for relationship problems?

Couples therapy may be a good fit when you recognize one or more of these situations. You don't have to wait until the difficulty becomes a crisis; sometimes addressing it early is exactly what keeps it from getting worse.

🔁

Recurring arguments

The same disagreements come up again and again, with no solution that works for both of you.

🗣️

Communication difficulties

Every attempt to talk about a sensitive subject ends in anger, defensiveness or silence.

🌫️

Emotional distance

You live together, but feel that the closeness, friendship and intimacy have faded.

💭

Feeling unappreciated

One or both of you feel that your efforts and needs go unseen.

⚖️

Gaps in expectations

Disagreements around parenting, money, sharing responsibilities at home, or time together.

🔀

Life changes

The birth of a child, a move, a career change or a period of heavy pressure that affects the relationship.

💗

Wanting to strengthen the relationship

Even when the relationship is stable, but you want to improve communication and know each other more deeply.

🤝

Not sure if now is the time?

A short intro call will help you understand whether couples therapy is right for you right now.

Recognized yourselves in one of these situations? It doesn't mean the relationship is lost. It means you can talk about it with someone from the outside. An intro call commits you to nothing.

The repeating pattern

Why do we keep coming back to the same fights?

One of the most frustrating things in a relationship is the feeling that you're having the same fight, just in different words.

One of you feels unheard, and so raises their voice. The other experiences it as criticism and pulls away or stops responding. The more the first tries to get attention, the more the second shuts down, and so a cycle forms that's hard to break out of.

It isn't always a lack of love or a lack of desire to be close. Sometimes these are reaction patterns that have set in over the years, and each of you acts from them almost automatically. In the process, you learn to recognize the moment the familiar pattern starts, understand what triggers it, and explore other ways to respond.

Instead of staying with the content of the last fight, we look at the sequence: what started the conversation, how each of you interpreted it, and at what moment the connection was lost.
Patterns that damage communication

Four patterns that can wear down how couples talk

Relationship researcher Dr. John Gottman identified four negative communication patterns he called "the Four Horsemen." They aren't a verdict, but when they become a regular part of how you talk, they make it harder for a couple to handle disagreements.

🗯️

Personal criticism

Instead of talking about a specific behavior that bothers you, you attack your partner's character: "You never think about me" instead of explaining which behavior hurt.

🙄

Contempt

Mockery, humiliation, scornful imitation or eye-rolling send a message of disrespect and can deeply damage the sense of safety in the relationship.

🛡️

Defensiveness

When every remark is taken as an attack, we rush to justify ourselves or blame back, instead of trying to understand what's bothering the other person.

🚪

Stonewalling

One partner stops responding, withdraws or avoids the conversation. Sometimes it's a response to feeling emotionally flooded, but the other partner may experience it as rejection.

Recognizing these patterns is an important step: it lets you stop an automatic reaction and choose a different way to handle the conversation.

Everyday tools

How do you improve communication as a couple day to day?

Changing how you communicate doesn't require grand gestures. Often it's the small habits that make it possible to build a more respectful dialogue, even between partners who have talked to each other the same way for years.

⏰

Choose the right time to talk

A meaningful conversation that starts when one of you is rushing to work, tired or upset can end in frustration. Pick a time when you're both free to listen, and if one of you feels flooded, agree on a break and come back at a set time.

💬

Talk about the feeling, don't blame

There's a difference between "You never consider me" and "I was hurt when we made that decision without talking about it together." The second sentence describes an event and a feeling, and makes it possible to understand without feeling attacked.

🔮

Don't expect mind reading

Even in a long relationship, you can't always know what your partner is going through. A clear request like "It's important to me that we set aside half an hour in the evening to talk without phones" works better than expecting the other person to notice the need on their own.

⏸️

Stop a fight in time

You don't have to keep arguing just because the conversation started. You can say, "I feel like we're starting to hurt each other, let's take a break and come back when we've calmed down," as long as the break doesn't turn into permanent avoidance.

🌼

Remember the good things too

Communication as a couple doesn't only happen during fights. Expressing appreciation, showing interest, shared humor and small gestures are also linked in Gottman's research to relationship stability and satisfaction.

One practical tool is rephrasing the same message from criticism into a request, saying what matters without attacking the other person:

"You never think about me"

→

"When I told you I'd had a hard day and the conversation ended right away, I felt alone. I'd like you to show interest and ask what happened"

"You never consider me"

→

"I was hurt when we made that decision without talking about it together. It's important to me that we make decisions like that together"

"You never help around the house"

→

"When I finish my workday and there are still lots of chores left, I feel overwhelmed. It's important to me that we share the responsibility in a way that works for both of us"

Tools from the world of NLP

Couples therapy with an NLP approach - how can it help?

The NLP approach focuses on the connection between thoughts, language, emotions and behavior patterns. The way we interpret our partner's words and actions has a direct effect on how we react.

Your partner didn't answer a message

→

“Maybe they're just busy” instead of “they don't care about me” - examining the automatic interpretation before it sparks hurt or anger

Using NLP tools, you can work on recognizing automatic interpretations, examining beliefs and communication habits, and developing more conscious responses. The guidance can focus on several key areas:

  • Recognizing thought patterns and reactions that repeat in the relationship.
  • Improving the ability to express feelings and needs clearly and respectfully.
  • Developing listening and an understanding of your partner's point of view.
  • Dealing with automatic reactions of anger, withdrawal or defensiveness.
  • Strengthening the sense of closeness and cooperation.

It's important to distinguish between using NLP tools as part of couples guidance and clinical couples therapy provided by professionals with dedicated training. Which process fits depends on the nature of the difficulties and the couple's needs.

How it works

How does the couples guidance process work?

Every couple comes with a story of their own. Some couples want to deal with a specific problem, while others feel that something in the relationship has changed but find it hard to say what.

1

Getting acquainted and clarifying the difficulties

We start by getting to know each other and clarifying what's on your minds. It's important to make room for both points of view, even when they differ.

2

Identifying the recurring pattern

We identify communication patterns that repeat and look at the needs behind the reactions: not who's right, but what each of you is really asking for.

3

Practicing new ways

We practice new ways of handling everyday situations, so the change doesn't stay in the room but shows up at home too.

4

Checking what's changing

Are conversations opening up? Are agreements being kept? We pause along the way to check progress and adjust what comes next to your pace.

The pace of progress varies from couple to couple and depends on how complex the difficulties are, on both partners' willingness to take part, and on the goals you set together.

Even after years

Can a relationship improve even after years of distance?

When distance has gone on for a long time, it's easy to feel you've already tried everything. But sometimes you've tried again and again to solve the problems in the same way, and so you've reached the same result.

Change often begins with the ability to look at the relationship from a different angle: understanding what lies behind the anger, what each of you wants to receive, and which habits you can start to change.

There's no way to guarantee that every relationship will improve or that every gap can be bridged. But when there's mutual desire and willingness to work together, you can explore new possibilities for communication and closeness.

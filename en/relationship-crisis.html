Relationship Crisis: How Do You Know if It's a Rough Patch or the Real Thing?

Conversations shrinking and distance growing? How to tell a rough patch from a real relationship crisis, and when to seek help. Read more.

November 11, 2026 6-minute read By Alon Shemesh

This article is written for women and men alike.

There are times when a relationship simply feels different. Conversations get shorter, patience wears thin, small things start to bother you more than before, and sometimes even when you're home together you feel far apart.

Maybe you tell yourself it's just a busy stretch. Work is demanding, the kids need attention, and there's an endless list of things to get done. But as time goes on, a question becomes hard to ignore: is this a temporary difficulty that will pass once the pressure eases, or are we already in the middle of a relationship crisis?

The answer isn't always clear. A relationship changes over the years, and not every period of distance points to a deep problem. That said, when the difficulties become a fixed part of the relationship, it's important to understand what changed and what can be done differently.

What's the difference between a rough patch and a relationship crisis?

A rough patch in a relationship can show up after external events such as a move, the birth of a child, financial strain, a change at work, or a complex family situation.

During times like these, partners may be more tired, less available, and find it hard to make time for each other. Despite the tension, there's usually still a sense that you can talk, lean on each other, and find your way back to closeness.

A relationship crisis, by contrast, can develop when the difficulty isn't tied only to external circumstances, but also to the patterns of communication and behavior that have formed between the partners.

Sometimes the pressure has already eased, but the distance remains. The same arguments keep coming back, one partner feels unseen, and the other feels that every attempt to get closer ends in criticism.

In situations like these, couples therapy can help partners understand the dynamic that has formed between them, identify patterns that keep repeating, and explore how to build a different kind of communication.

Why don't relationship crises always start with a big fight?

When you think of a relationship in crisis, it's easy to picture a dramatic event like infidelity, a breach of trust, or an especially big fight. But quite a few crises actually develop quietly.

At first you stop sharing the small things that happened during the day. Then you give up on conversations because you don't have the energy for another argument. Later, each of you finds your own ways to cope with the difficulty without involving the other.

From the outside, life goes on as usual. You go to work, take care of the kids, run the household, and even go out to family events now and then.

But on the inside, something shifts. Instead of feeling that you're facing life together, a sense grows that each of you is running your own separate world.

The difficulty isn't necessarily a lack of love. Sometimes the love is still there, but the way to express it, to ask for closeness, or to talk about what's bothering you no longer works the way it used to.

What happens when each partner experiences the crisis differently?

One of the challenges of a relationship in crisis is that the two partners don't always feel the difficulty with the same intensity.

One may experience significant emotional distance and want to talk about it again and again. The other may feel that everything is more or less fine, or prefer to focus on routine and hope things will work themselves out.

This gap can create a frustrating cycle. The more one partner tries to talk, the more pressured the other feels and pulls away. The more they pull away, the lonelier the first one feels, and the harder they try to connect.

In the end, both may feel misunderstood.

It's important to remember that there's no single right way to experience a crisis. Different people express emotional distress in different ways. The question isn't who feels more or who's right, but whether the partners can acknowledge that something in the relationship needs attention.

Can a relationship crisis become an opportunity for change?

A crisis isn't necessarily the end of the relationship. Sometimes it signals that the ways of coping that worked in the past no longer fit the current situation.

For example, a couple used to making decisions spontaneously may find that after having children a clearer division of responsibility is needed. Partners who were used to spending a lot of time together may need new ways to keep their closeness during a busy period.

In other cases, the crisis reveals needs that haven't had enough room: a wish to feel valued, a need for independence, difficulty asking for support, or a sense that personal needs have been pushed aside.

When you manage to identify these needs and talk about them, the crisis can become a starting point for change.

That said, not every crisis leads to repairing the relationship, and not every relationship is worth preserving at any cost. In relationships that involve violence, threats, coercion, or control, safety must come first and you should turn to appropriate professional help.

How do you start coping with a relationship crisis?

You don't always have to start with big decisions. Sometimes it's precisely small changes in how you go about daily life that create new movement in the relationship.

1. Stop looking for who started it

When every conversation turns into an attempt to prove who's to blame, it's hard to move forward. Instead of focusing only on the latest incident, try to understand what keeps repeating and what each of you feels inside that pattern.

2. Talk about the need, not just the behavior

Instead of saying "you're never home," you can explain "I feel like we hardly spend time together, and I miss it." The message is still clear, but it lets the other side understand the need behind the frustration.

3. Set aside time for conversation that isn't about running the home

When every conversation centers on the kids, the bills, and the to-do list, it's easy to forget the friendly, personal side of the relationship. Even a short, regular time to talk without distractions can help recreate a sense of closeness.

4. Notice what still works in the relationship

During a crisis it's easy to focus only on what isn't working. It's worth also asking what still connects you, in which moments you feel close, and what you'd like to preserve.

5. Don't expect immediate change

Relationship patterns built over months or years don't change after a single conversation. Change takes willingness, practice, and sometimes professional guidance too.

When is it worth considering therapy for relationship difficulties?

You don't have to reach a breaking point to seek help.

When the difficulties keep coming back, when conversations don't lead to understanding, or when the emotional distance keeps deepening, it's worth considering guidance.

At the Nefeshlava clinic of Alon Shemesh in Rehovot, we work on identifying patterns of communication and behavior, understanding each partner's needs, and developing practical tools to improve the relationship.

The process combines tools from NLP and personal coaching, while addressing the couple's specific difficulties and the goals they're hoping to achieve.

The goal isn't to promise that every crisis will end in a return to how things were, but to allow a different way of looking at the relationship and making decisions out of understanding and clarity.

Frequently asked questions about relationship crises

Does every relationship crisis mean the relationship is about to end?

No. Crises can appear even in stable, long-lasting relationships. The main question is how the partners cope with the difficulty, whether there's a willingness to listen, and whether change is possible in the patterns that are hurting the relationship.

Can you work through a relationship crisis without couples therapy?

Some couples manage to get through hard periods through open conversations, changing habits, and reinvesting in the relationship. When the difficulties persist or attempts to change the situation don't succeed, professional guidance can help.

What do you do if only one partner feels the relationship is in crisis?

It helps to start with a non-blaming conversation, explain what's missing in the relationship, and try to understand how the other person experiences the situation. Even when there's no agreement about how serious the difficulty is, you can start exploring what each of you needs in order to feel better in the relationship.

You don't have to wait for the distance to become the norm

A relationship doesn't stay the same over the years. People change, needs evolve, and life brings new challenges.

Sometimes a relationship crisis is the result of an especially busy period, and sometimes it's a signal that it's time to change something in the way partners communicate and manage life together.

Instead of waiting for things to work themselves out or assuming the relationship can no longer change, you can start with an honest conversation about what's missing, what hurts, and what each of you would want to happen differently.

Because even when a relationship is going through a complex period, you can still consider where you want to go from here.

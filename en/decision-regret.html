Difficulty making decisions: why do we keep regretting even after we've already chosen?

You chose, decided and closed the matter, and still your mind keeps going back and forth, checking whether you got it wrong. Why difficulty making decisions doesn't end at the moment of choice, and how to break out of the cycle of regret and doubt.

October 28, 2026 4-minute read By Alon Shemesh

This article is written for women and men alike.

You chose a new job, but you keep checking other openings. You decided to end a relationship, but you can't stop thinking you may have made a mistake. Even after a simple purchase, you find yourself checking prices, reviews and other options all over again.

On the face of it, the decision has already been made. So why does your mind keep debating?

Difficulty making decisions doesn't always end at the moment we choose. For some people, it's precisely after the choice that a new cycle of doubt, regret and fear begins, the fear that there was a better way.

Why is it so hard to stop thinking about the option we didn't choose?

Every meaningful decision involves giving something up. When we choose one path, we leave other options behind, and it isn't always easy to accept that.

People who struggle with decisions may believe that every situation has one perfect choice, and that if they just think hard enough, they'll find it.

But in reality, even a good decision can come with downsides, uncertainty or results we didn't foresee.

When we expect ourselves to choose without ever getting it wrong, any difficulty that comes up after the decision can be read as proof that we chose badly.

That's how an ongoing sense of insecurity takes hold, even when the choice was sensible and right for the circumstances.

4 signs that regret has become a fixed pattern

Not every recurring thought about a decision is a problem. Sometimes it's important to revisit choices, especially when new information comes in. The difficulty begins when going over it again doesn't lead to a useful change, but only deepens the doubt.

  • Seeking approval from others: even after you've made a decision, you keep consulting friends and family to make sure you didn't get it wrong.
  • Comparing yourself to others: every success of someone who chose differently makes you wonder whether you missed an opportunity.
  • Difficulty enjoying the choice: instead of investing in the path you chose, you're busy thinking about what might have been.
  • Dreading the next decision: the experience of regret strengthens the fear of getting it wrong again, and the next dilemma becomes even harder.

How does fear of regret affect future decisions?

When we regret things over and over, we may begin to doubt our ability to trust ourselves.

Someone who regretted changing jobs may pass up a new professional opportunity. Someone who felt regret after a breakup may stay in a relationship that isn't right for them, just to avoid facing the doubts again.

Gradually, the fear of regret becomes a central factor in making decisions.

Instead of asking "What's right for me?", we start asking "How can I be sure I won't regret this?".

But there's no way to guarantee in advance that we'll never have regrets. What we can do is learn to make decisions with awareness, even when the outcome is uncertain.

How do you stop re-examining every decision?

The first step is to tell the difference between a thought that brings new information and a thought that repeats the same doubt.

Ask yourself: did I discover something I didn't know when I made the choice, or am I simply finding it hard to accept the uncertainty?

You can also go back to the reasons you made the decision in the first place. What mattered to you at that moment? What options did you have? Did you act on the information that was available?

Instead of judging the decision only by its outcome, try to look at the way it was made as well.

When indecision and regret keep showing up in different areas of life, it's worth considering help with difficulty making decisions or tailored professional guidance. For those looking for practical tools to change thinking patterns and cope with the fear of making mistakes, coaching for decision making can be a way to work on the difficulty and build more confidence in personal choices.

Personal coaching for decision making: learning to trust ourselves

Decision making is a skill you can develop. It doesn't require complete certainty, but rather the ability to weigh options, recognize your personal needs and move forward even when there's no perfect answer.

Personal coaching for decision making focuses on identifying limiting thinking patterns, understanding the fears behind the indecision and developing tools for coping with doubt and uncertainty.

At Alon Shemesh's Nefeshlava clinic, the work combines tools from NLP and personal coaching, with the aim of strengthening your sense of capability and practicing new ways of thinking and acting.

The goal isn't to guarantee that every decision will be right, but to develop the ability to choose, take responsibility and keep moving forward without staying trapped in an endless cycle of regret.

Because sometimes the meaningful change isn't in the decision we make, but in the way we learn to live with it.

Treating anxiety, fears and phobias - learning to cope and regain a sense of control

When anxiety, fear or a phobia starts running parts of your life, causing avoidance and disrupting your routine, it's important to know there are ways to cope. NLP-based emotional support that helps you understand your reactions, develop tools and bring back calm, at a pace that suits you.

Before we begin

When fear starts running parts of your life

Sometimes fear shows up in the most ordinary moments. Before a drive, an important meeting, a conversation with someone close or a decision you need to make. You know there isn't always a real danger, and still your body goes on alert, your heart races and the thoughts won't stop. In other cases, anxiety stays with you throughout the day, even when everything looks fine from the outside.

When anxiety, fears or phobias start affecting your decisions, leading you to avoid certain situations or disrupting your daily routine, it's important to know there are ways to cope.

Treating anxiety, fears and phobias through emotional support can help you understand your reactions, identify thought and behavior patterns and develop tools for coping with fear and anxiety, at a pace that suits you.

Let's start with the basics

What is the difference between fear, anxiety and phobia?

Fear is a natural response of body and mind to a situation perceived as dangerous. It is an important protective mechanism meant to warn us of danger and help us react quickly. When we face a real threat, fear can protect us.

Anxiety, on the other hand, can appear even when there is no immediate danger. It shows up as ongoing worry, thoughts about what might happen and a sense of tension or alertness that's hard to shake off. Anxiety is sometimes accompanied by physical symptoms such as a rapid heartbeat, sweating, chest pressure, trouble sleeping or a choking sensation.

A phobia is an intense, persistent fear that usually centers on a specific situation, object or activity. Sometimes the person is aware that the intensity of the fear doesn't match the actual danger, yet still struggles to control their emotional and physical reaction.

The differences between these states matter, but what they share is the feeling that fear is starting to run parts of your life.

What it looks like

Common types of fear and anxiety in adults

Fears can take different forms and affect many areas of life. Some are linked to familiar experiences from the past, and others develop gradually, with no clear reason. Common fears include:

✈️

Fear of driving or flying

Worry about losing control, about an accident or about a situation where it's hard to get away from the source of the fear.

🏙️

Fear of heights or enclosed spaces

A strong emotional and physical reaction in certain situations, even when there is no real danger.

🎤

Fear of public speaking and social situations

Worry about criticism, embarrassment, rejection or being judged by others.

🧭

Fear of failure or change

Difficulty making decisions, advancing in your career or facing new situations.

🫀

Fear of illness or loss

Ongoing worry about your own health or the well-being of people close to you.

🌫️

Unexplained fears

Feelings of fear or threat that appear even when it's hard to pinpoint what exactly triggers them.

Not every fear points to a problem that requires treatment. But when fear persists, intensifies or limits your freedom of choice, it's worth considering professional help.

In everyday life

How do anxiety and fears affect everyday life?

Anxiety doesn't always show from the outside. You can keep working, run a family, see friends and function as usual, while at the same time experiencing inner restlessness, recurring thoughts or a feeling that something bad is about to happen.

Sometimes anxiety shows up as trouble falling asleep, ongoing fatigue or a need to check again and again that everything is okay. In other cases it leads you to avoid places, activities or opportunities that used to be a natural part of life.

You may be skipping a trip because of a fear of driving, putting off an important conversation for fear of the other person's reaction, or avoiding social gatherings because you feel insecure. Over time, avoidance can shrink your sense of independence and increase the fear of facing the next challenge.

The avoidance cycle

Why do we avoid what scares us?

Avoidance is a natural response to fear. When we stay away from a situation that triggers anxiety, we usually feel immediate relief. The body calms down, the tension drops and we feel we've managed to protect ourselves. But sometimes that temporary relief actually strengthens the fear.

For example, if you're afraid of driving on the highway and take an alternative route every time, you may feel safer in that moment. But the longer you keep avoiding the drive, the stronger the fear may grow of the next time you have to face it.

This is the avoidance cycle: fear leads you to stay away from the situation, staying away brings temporary relief, but it prevents the chance to learn that you can handle the situation differently.

Coping with fears doesn't require you to fight yourself or plunge all at once into a situation that scares you. Sometimes the path begins with understanding your reaction, developing self-compassion and taking gradual steps that match your personal ability.

Tools for the here and now

Coping with anxiety and fear in everyday life

Alongside the emotional process, there are simple tools that can help with anxiety when it shows up during the day. Slow, comfortable breathing, moderate physical activity, regular sleep and reducing overload can all contribute to a sense of balance. Noticing the thoughts that appear during anxiety can also help you understand what triggers the reaction.

When you feel afraid, try asking yourself: is there a real danger right now, or is my mind focusing on a scenario that might happen?

It's also important to remember that you don't always need to make the fear disappear in order to keep going. Sometimes coping with fear begins with the ability to acknowledge it, let it be present and choose how to respond. When it comes to a significant phobia or anxiety that leads to widespread avoidance, it's recommended to build up coping gradually with appropriate professional support.

Honestly and responsibly

When is it worth seeking treatment for anxiety and fears?

Not every feeling of fear or worry calls for treatment. Fear and anxiety are a natural part of life, especially in times of change, uncertainty or overload.

That said, it's worth considering help when you feel the anxiety keeps coming back, disrupts your sleep, affects your relationships or makes you give up things that matter to you. Even when you experience unexplained fears, ongoing alertness or a sense that it's hard to calm down, talking with a professional can be a meaningful step toward understanding the difficulty.

In cases of severe anxiety, frequent panic attacks or significant impairment in functioning, it's important to seek diagnosis and treatment from a qualified mental health professional. New or unusual physical symptoms also require medical evaluation.
